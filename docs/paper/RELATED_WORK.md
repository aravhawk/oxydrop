# Related work

**Camera oximetry and hypoxemia screening.** Smartphone oxygen sensing predates OxyDrop. Ding, Nassehi, and Larson trained camera-based CNNs in a 39-participant breath-holding study using two phones [@ding2019]. Hoffman et al. subsequently evaluated an unmodified phone during controlled inspired-oxygen reduction in six participants, covering reference SpO₂ of 70–100%, with subject-held-out evaluation; their reported MAE was 5.00 percentage points, with 81% sensitivity and 79% specificity for SpO₂ below 90% [@hoffman2022]. Their reference was a transfer-standard pulse oximeter, not direct arterial co-oximetry. Bui et al.'s PhO₂ instead used an optical attachment for wavelength separation and chromophore compensation [@bui2020]. Tayfur and Afacan compared a Galaxy S8's built-in sensor with clinical references in 101 patients; this is evidence about dedicated phone sensor hardware, not ordinary RGB-camera performance [@tayfur2019]. Results across these acquisition modes and saturation ranges are not interchangeable. OxyDrop's difference, implemented in PR #6, is a baseline-conditioned hybrid of an explicit physiological chain and a compact multitask camera CNN, returning a screening state with abstention; neither camera SpO₂ nor CNN-based low-oxygen screening is new, and in OxyDrop's own leave-one-person-out test on six Hoffman people the hybrid tied the logistic chain rather than beating it (time-matched balanced accuracy 0.779 [0.638, 0.831] nested against 0.796 [0.635, 0.856]).

**Optical priors, pulse extraction, and calibration.** Camera pulse measurement is established: Verkruysse et al. recovered remote pulse signals under ambient light, and Scully et al. extracted cardiac intervals, respiration, and oxygen-related changes from fingertip phone recordings [@verkruysse2008; @scully2012]. For smartphone oxygen sensing, Tian et al. extended ratio-of-ratios to multiple RGB channels with heart-rate-adaptive filtering, while Mathew et al. designed optophysiology-inspired neural networks for noncontact hand videos [@tian2022; @mathew2023]. Thus, neither physiological priors nor combining optical knowledge with neural estimation is novel. Moreover, Xuan et al. showed why tone mapping and sensor offsets impair camera PPG and developed device calibration [@xuan2023]; subtracting a user's baseline does not correct those hardware nonlinearities. OxyDrop supplies beat-level red/green AC/DC ratios and their ratio as explicit temporal input channels alongside learned waveform features, then conditions predictions on an initial user baseline; this exact representation and adaptation mechanism, rather than the underlying optical formula or HR estimator, is the distinction. The ablation found no measured gain from the physics channels (P1 -0.008, inside the seed SD of 0.019), whereas removing baseline conditioning lowered P1 by 0.028.

**Datasets and the limits of transfer.** MTHS provides 62 smartphone fingertip recordings with pulse-oximeter HR and SpO₂ annotations at 1 Hz; its associated work reports compact 3.3k–53k-parameter vital-sign networks and TFLite deployment [@samavati2026]. Small models and mobile inference therefore already have close precedents. BUT PPG v2 provides 3,888 ten-second smartphone recordings from 50 people, ECG-derived HR references, and quality labels, making it useful for pulse and signal-quality learning rather than evidence of controlled hypoxemia performance [@butppg2024]. OpenOximetry supplies a common data model and restricted-access laboratory/clinical data supporting arterial co-oximetry, oximeter readings, PPG, and skin-color metadata [@fong2025; @openox2025]. It is not a paired smartphone RGB desaturation dataset by default: actual waveform modality, wavelength availability, timing, and label provenance must be checked. ABG warm-up must target measured SaO₂, not PaO₂ or an oximeter's SpO₂, and sparse blood samples cannot silently become independent beat labels. OxyDrop uses a staged curriculum that assigns these heterogeneous sources distinct roles: unlabeled representation learning, ABG-anchored desaturation warm-up, and smartphone-domain fine-tuning, rather than treating every source as equivalent camera-oximetry supervision. In OxyDrop, removing the OpenOximetry warm-up lowered P1 from 0.739 to 0.577 (the largest effect in the ablation, with overlapping person-level intervals), and the warm-up data are contact red/infrared PPG, so they say nothing about arterial validation of phone output. OpenOximetry and MTHS are restricted-licence sources, and the weights trained on them are shipped for non-commercial research and education use with attribution (`DATA_LICENSES.md`).

**Self-supervision, multitask learning, and hybrid models.** PPG representation learning is already mature enough to rule out broad first-of-kind claims. Apple's wearable-biosignal work uses participant-level positive pairs, augmentations, and contrastive momentum training on Apple Watch PPG and ECG from approximately 141,000 participants [@abbaspourazad2024]. PaPaGei learns participant- and morphology-aware PPG representations from public data at scale [@pillai2025]. Even smartphone contrastive vital estimation has precedent in Mehmood et al.'s Vitals-CLIP, alongside CNN and transformer approaches [@mehmood2023]. These systems differ in sensors, objectives, and supervision; their existence does not establish transfer to low-SpO₂ fingertip RGB recordings. Likewise, learning a combiner from out-of-fold base-model predictions is established stacked generalization [@wolpert1992]. OxyDrop's candidate contribution is the particular sequence of SimCLR-style contrastive pretraining, arterial-label warm-up, baseline-conditioned smartphone fine-tuning, and subject-nested stacking of the v1 logistic chain with a 167,588-parameter multitask CNN (three seeds). The pre-registered comparison against the best baseline was a tie, not a win: the chain refit with the same levers and no CNN has the higher point estimate (0.816 against 0.779 nested for the hybrid), so any benefit of the sequence is unproven.

**Uncertainty and selective medical prediction.** Conformal prediction provides marginal set coverage under exchangeability [@angelopoulos2022]; medical applications already exist [@vazquez2022]. A binary prediction set can yield three user-facing actions: a singleton normal set, a singleton low-oxygen set, or abstention when the set is ambiguous or empty. Signal-quality rejection is a separate mechanism. Set coverage is not the same as accuracy among answered readings, sensitivity to low oxygen, or subgroup safety. Repeated overlapping windows from one person are dependent, and six participants provide very few independent units for calibration and evaluation. Subject-held-out model selection alone does not establish conformal validity. OxyDrop connects calibrated uncertainty to the specific actions “normal,” “possible low, re-check,” and “unsure, re-measure,” reporting coverage and missed-low-oxygen rates together; its distinction is this application and evaluation of abstention, not invention of conformal prediction. Measured result: class-conditional (Mondrian) set coverage is near target when pooled over six people and fails per person (one held-out person has 51.3% of hypoxic windows called Normal), and at alpha 0.10 the chain-only control answers more windows (92.5% against 74.5%) at similar accuracy (90.3% against 89.5%).

**Bias, reference standards, and clinical scope.** Sjoding et al. found substantially more occult hypoxemia among Black than White patients despite apparently reassuring pulse-oximeter readings; Fawzy et al. associated pulse-oximetry discrepancies with delayed or unrecognized COVID-19 treatment eligibility [@sjoding2020; @fawzy2022]. These observational race/ethnicity comparisons motivate investigation but do not make race a direct measure of pigmentation. The FDA's January 2025 pulse-oximeter guidance is explicitly a nonbinding draft, not a final approval standard; it proposes improved performance evaluation across skin pigmentation and clearer labeling [@fda2025]. A model trained against biased oximeter labels can inherit their error, while ABG supervision in a different sensor domain does not itself establish fairness on phones. Neither baseline adaptation nor abstention guarantees equal performance. OxyDrop therefore frames its contribution as an auditable research screen with pre-registered subject-disjoint comparisons and, so far, only one stratified analysis: a skin-tone analysis on OpenOximetry contact-sensor data (Monk 1-3 and 4-6 groups, others suppressed below five people). Pigmentation-, device-, and saturation-stratified analyses on phone video are not done (one dark-skinned Hoffman participant, no phone pigmentation measurement), and the app is not validated on any phone model. It is not a clinically validated replacement for arterial measurement and has not demonstrated a solution to skin-tone bias.
